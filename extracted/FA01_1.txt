Abstract
A method for developing optimized point-to-point motion
profiles to achieve fast motions with minimum vibration is
presented.  The proposed approach uses the well-known s-
curve motion profiles, but optimizes the selection of the
ramp-up (and ramp-down) time.  The selection of ramp-up
time is based on a frequency analysis that minimizes the
excitation energy of the input forcing function at the system
natural frequency.  Simulation results on a lightly-damped
system undergoing point-to-point motions demonstrate that
the proposed approach decreases residual vibration by
almost an order of magnitude over other approaches, even
when the actual natural frequency is in error by 10%.
1. Introduction
The challenge of achieving fast motions without residual
vibration 
pervades 
many 
modern 
manufacturing
applications.  It is well known that simple trapezoidal
velocity profiles, in which the machine is accelerated to a
constant velocity at a constant acceleration and decelerated
to rest at a constant deceleration, can achieve fast motions.
However, since most real systems exhibit some compliance,
these reference profiles tend to excite vibrations that require
time to dissipate for the final position to be reached with
desired precision.  As a result, s-curve velocity profiles have
been proposed to reduce the tendency to excite system
vibration [1,2].  In these profiles, the trajectory ramps up to
peak acceleration and ramps down to constant velocity,
thereby producing an s-shaped velocity profile.  Depending
on the duration of this ramp-up time, the resulting motion
can be both fast and precise.  However, to date, no
systematic method for optimally tuning this parameter has
been presented.  This paper will extend earlier work [3,4,5]
on shaping the frequency content of the input commands to
optimally select the ramp-up time for s-curve velocity
profiles.  Both rest-to-velocity and rest-to-rest simulation
results for a lightly-damped system will be provided.
Typical motion profiles for trapezoidal and s-curve
velocities are shown in Fig. 1.  The input is typically a force
or torque command.  Notice that the trapezoidal velocity
profiles exhibit large spikes in jerk, whereas the s-curve
profiles exhibit finite jerk spread out over a period of time.
It is primarily this quality that contributes to lower vibration
for the s-curve profiles.  However, it can be shown that even
trapezoidal profiles can achieve vibration-free motion for
highly flexible systems if the acceleration and deceleration
periods are properly chosen.  Thus, the ultimate vibration
performance depends on more than just the presence or
absence of large spikes in jerk.  In addition, without further
analysis, it is impossible to determine an optimal selection
of the ramp-up time (and ramp-down time) for the s-curve
profiles that will achieve both fast motions and minimum
residual vibration.

the nominal natural frequency for which the optimized input

shown in Fig. 9, again for all three input profiles.  Although
the residual acceleration amplitudes for the optimized inputs
are higher than for the ideal case of Fig. 8, they are still
significantly lower than the amplitudes for the other two
inputs for a major portion of the range of ωntr /2π values.
The improvement is on the order of 20 dB at small values of
ωntr/2π, where system natural frequency is low and response
time is fast.
5. Conclusions
Motion control using the so-called s-curve velocity profile
has become a popular approach to achieving fast motions
with reduced residual vibration.  However, the ramp-up
time to reach peak acceleration for these forcing profiles has
never been optimized to take full advantage of the
vibration-reducing capability of this forcing function.  This
paper provides a method based on the frequency content of
the forcing function to select this ramp-up time so that both
response time and residual vibration amplitude are
minimized.  Open-loop simulations for a lightly-damped
system confirm that these optimized input profiles can
achieve superior performance, even when the actual system
natural frequency differs by 10 % from the frequency for
which the inputs were computed.  Vibration reduction of
almost an order of magnitude is possible, relative to non-
optimized inputs.  This improvement is most pronounced
for systems having low natural frequencies and requiring
fast motions.
